# NIST SP 800-63B-4

Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management. Revision 4,
published July 2025 (NIST lists the final release as 2025-07-31). It supersedes the
2020-03-02 edition of SP 800-63B.

Source: <https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html>
Publication record: <https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/63/b/4/final>
Verified: 2026-07-28

Companion volumes: SP 800-63 (framework), 800-63A (identity proofing), 800-63C (federation).

## Terminology change worth noting

Revision 4 says "password" throughout. "Memorized secret", the term used in the 2020
edition, is gone. If a project's policy document quotes "memorized secret" requirements, it
is quoting the old revision.

Likewise "verifier impersonation" was renamed. Section 3.2.5 calls it phishing.

## Passwords

Section 3.1.1, split into 3.1.1.1 (authenticators, the subscriber side) and 3.1.1.2
(verifiers, the code you write).

What 3.1.1.2 requires of a verifier:

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| Minimum length, password as the only factor | 15 characters |
| Minimum length, password as one factor of several | 8 characters |
| Maximum length accepted | at least 64 characters |
| Composition rules (upper, digit, symbol) | SHALL NOT be imposed |
| Periodic forced rotation | not required; rotate only on evidence of compromise |
| Blocklist check | compare against known commonly used, expected, or compromised values |
| Storage | salted and hashed with a suitable one-way KDF |
| Repeated guessing | see rate limiting, 3.2.2 |

The composition and rotation rules are prohibitions, not permissions. "Must contain a
symbol" is now a finding, not a control. It pushes users toward `Password1!` and blocks
passphrases that are stronger.

Blocklist rejection has to force a new choice and tell the user why. Silently truncating or
transforming the password fails the requirement and breaks the account later.

Appendix A (Strength of Passwords) carries the informative reasoning if you need to argue
the case with a compliance reviewer.

## Rate limiting

Section 3.2.2. Verifiers limit failed authentication attempts on an account. The document
sets an upper bound of 100 consecutive failed attempts before further attempts must be
prevented, and discusses mitigations so the limit does not become a denial-of-service lever
against the legitimate user.

Note the shape of that requirement: it is per account. It does not address one attempt each
against a million accounts from a rotating IP pool, which is what credential stuffing
actually looks like. Read it as a floor and add per-IP, per-ASN, and global-failure-rate
controls on top.

An authenticator disabled by rate limiting has to be rebound to the account before it works
again.

## Authenticator assurance levels

Chapter 2, with AAL1, AAL2, and AAL3 each specifying permitted authenticator types,
authenticator and verifier requirements, and reauthentication rules. Section 2.5 has a
non-normative summary table across the three.

Rough shape:

| AAL | Requires |
|---|---|
| AAL1 | single factor, any permitted type |
| AAL2 | two distinct factors; phishing-resistant options must be offered |
| AAL3 | hardware-based authenticator, phishing resistance required |

Cite the AAL you targeted. "We are AAL2" without a mapped set of authenticators is a claim,
not a finding.

## Phishing resistance

Section 3.2.5. Defined as preventing disclosure of an authenticator secret to an impostor
verifier, without relying on the user noticing anything. Two recognised mechanisms: channel
binding and verifier name binding.

The consequence people miss: anything the user types into a page is not phishing-resistant,
because manual entry does not bind the output to the channel. That rules out TOTP codes,
SMS codes, and push-approval prompts. WebAuthn qualifies through verifier name binding;
PIV/CAC through channel binding.

Required at AAL3. Must be offered at AAL2.

## SMS and the PSTN

Sections 3.1.3, 3.1.3.3, and 3.2.9. Out-of-band authentication over the public switched
telephone network is the one authenticator the document explicitly labels restricted.

What that means in practice for a CSP using SMS or voice:

- Offer at least one alternative authenticator that is not restricted
- Tell subscribers about the risk
- Include it in the risk assessment
- Keep a migration plan for the possibility that it stops being acceptable
- Consider fraud signals: device swap, SIM change, number porting, other abnormal behaviour
- Treat setting or changing the registered phone number as binding a new authenticator,
  with the same rigour as adding any other factor

Email is not a permitted out-of-band authenticator at all. A code emailed as part of
account recovery or address validation is a different thing — that is not an authentication
process, so it is not covered by the prohibition.

## Recovery and loss

Binding and recovery are Sections 4.1 and 4.2. Points that matter for code:

- There must be a mechanism to invalidate an authenticator immediately when the subscriber
  reports it lost
- Recovery codes are the standard example of look-up secrets: single-use, generated by the
  system, high entropy
- If the subscriber cannot reach the required AAL, the recovery procedure in 4.2 applies —
  recovery is a defined path, not an ad hoc support ticket

## Syncable authenticators

Appendix B is normative and covers passkeys that sync across a vendor's device fleet. Worth
reading before deciding whether a synced passkey satisfies your AAL target, because the
private key existing in more than one place changes the analysis.

## What this document does not do

It is US federal guidance for credential service providers. It says nothing about
authorization models, OAuth grant selection, session cookie attributes, or JWT validation.
For those, use ASVS V7 through V10 and RFC 9700. Do not stretch a 800-63B citation to cover
them.
